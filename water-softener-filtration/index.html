Strips versus a lab test

  • Test strips cost little, give a rough reading in a minute, and are fine for a first look at hardness.
  • A certified lab test measures hardness, iron, pH and more, and is the better basis for a purchase.
  • Run the tap for a minute before sampling so you are not testing water that sat in the pipe.
  • Test the cold side, since hot water can pick up minerals from the tank.

Results usually come as grains per gallon or milligrams per liter. One grain per gallon is about 17 milligrams per liter, and a plumber or lab can tell you how your reading compares with commonly used softness scales.

Signs your water may be hard

  • White crust on faucets, showerheads and the kettle.
  • Spots on dishes and glassware, even after the dishwasher.
  • Soap that does not lather, or a film on the tub.
  • Dry skin or stiff laundry.
  • A water heater that rumbles as scale builds up inside the tank.

These signs point toward hardness, but they are not proof. A test tells you whether to buy anything.

Be honest about what you want solved. If the complaint is scale on fixtures, a softener fits. If it is taste, a carbon filter or an under-sink unit fits. If you are worried about a specific contaminant, a lab test comes first and the equipment follows.

What to ask before you buy

Filtration products differ a lot, and marketing claims are loose. Ask these questions of any seller, including us.

  1. 1What does it remove, specifically?Ask for the contaminants by name, not "purifies water."
  2. 2Is it independently certified?Look for NSF/ANSI standards, such as 44 for softeners, 53 for filters and 58 for reverse osmosis, and ask to see the listing for the exact model.
  3. 3What does it cost to run?Salt, filter cartridges, membranes and water used for regeneration all count.
  4. 4What maintenance does it need?Ask how often filters change and who does it.
  5. 5How big is it?Ask for the dimensions and the drain, water and power it needs.

How installation works

A whole-house unit goes on the main water line, usually near where the line enters the house and ahead of the water heater. A plumber installs a bypass valve so you can isolate the unit for service, connects inlet and outlet, and runs a drain line for the backwash or regeneration water, to a floor drain or standpipe with an air gap. Most softeners also need a nearby outlet for the control head.

Installation also means checking pressure, flow and the pipe the unit connects to. Plumbing permits in Sapulpa are separate from building permits and go through Building Inspections for licensed, city-registered contractors, so ask City of Sapulpa Building Inspections whether your installation needs one. The plumber can also fold this into a broader plumbing installation project.

Where it can go

Garages, utility rooms and basements work well. Avoid spots that freeze, and leave space around the tank for salt refills and service. A tankless water heater may benefit from softened water because scale is hard on narrow heat exchangers.

Maintenance you will actually do

  • Check the salt level monthly and top it up before it runs low.
  • Break up any salt bridge that forms a crust over empty space.
  • Replace filter cartridges on the schedule in the manual.
  • Clean the brine tank once a year or as the manual says.
  • Check the bypass valve and the drain line for leaks.

When you do not need one

Not every house benefits. If a test shows soft or moderate water, a softener adds cost and maintenance for little gain. If the only complaint is chlorine taste, a small carbon filter may be enough. People on low-sodium diets should talk with a doctor before drinking softened water. Homes on septic should check the manufacturer's guidance on regeneration discharge.

Before spending money on equipment, rule out a plumbing problem. Poor pressure or discolored water can come from a failing pipe, in which case water line repair may be the real fix.
