Under-Slab Plumbing Rough-In in Sapulpa, OK: What Goes In Before the Pour

Once concrete covers a pipe, every fix means a jackhammer. This guide explains what gets set in the ground first and what you should see before the truck arrives.

Call (855) 575-2890

Under-slab plumbing rough-in is the pipework installed in the ground before a concrete floor is poured. In Sapulpa, OK that means the drain lines, vent connections and sometimes water supply lines are laid in trenches, tested and inspected while they are still easy to reach. After the pour, the only way back to those pipes is to cut through concrete. For a new house or an addition, this is the one plumbing stage you cannot redo cheaply.

Start with the drawing, not the shovel

Rough-in begins on paper. The plumber works from the floor plan and marks where every drain must come up through the slab: toilets, tubs, shower pans, floor drains, the kitchen sink, the washer box. Each mark has to line up with the framing that will go on top, because a toilet drain that lands two inches inside a wall cannot be nudged later.

Before any trench opens, walk the plan with the person who is framing. Confirm the toilet centerline, the tub length, the shower size and which way the vanity doors swing. Ten minutes of checking here prevents the most common slab mistake, a drain in the wrong place.

What actually gets placed before the pour

The trench work covers more than people expect. A typical under-slab package includes the following pieces.

  • Drain pipe in PVC or ABS, sloped toward the sewer connection (smaller drain lines usually need about a quarter inch of fall per foot, and your adopted code has the final word)
  • Stub-ups where fixtures will sit, capped so wet concrete cannot get inside
  • Cleanout fittings that bring access to the surface
  • Water supply lines, if they are being run under the floor, often PEX inside a protective sleeve
  • Sleeves or wrapping where pipe passes through concrete, so the pipe can move slightly without scraping
  • Bedding material, usually sand or fine fill, around the pipe so a sharp rock does not press on it

What stays out of the slab

Gas lines, electrical conduit and the plumbing vent stack that goes up through the roof belong to separate trades or later stages. Vents that connect to under-slab drains are mostly built in the walls. Ask the plumber which pieces are going in the ground and which come at the second visit.

The inspection stage

Between trench work and concrete there is a short window for testing. The plumber seals the open ends and fills the drain system with water, or pressurizes it with air, then watches for any drop. Supply lines get their own pressure test. A leak that shows up now is a repaired joint. A leak that shows up after the pour is a slab leak.

Inspection is separate from the plumber's own test. A city inspector looks at pipe size, slope, support, joints and the test result before the trench is covered. Schedule that visit early, because concrete crews and pump trucks are booked days ahead and a failed or missed inspection pushes the whole pour.

Why the details matter ten years later

Pipe that is level, supported and sleeved tends to behave. Pipe that sags between supports collects debris, and pipe that touches rock or rebar can wear through. Anything that ends up needing service later gets harder because the line is under four inches or more of concrete.

That is why slab leak repair is so much more involved than fixing a leak under a sink. The plumber has to locate the line first, then choose between breaking the floor, rerouting the pipe through the attic or walls, or lining the pipe. Good rough-in work reduces the odds that you ever need that conversation.

Photograph everything

Before the concrete arrives, take pictures of the open trench from several angles and measure from fixed points such as exterior walls to each stub-up and cleanout. Store the photos with your closing papers. Years from now, a leak specialist can use those measurements to cut in the right spot instead of guessing.

Sapulpa permit and inspection notes

The City of Sapulpa Building Inspections page says plumbing permits are separate from building permits. They are requested by contractors who are licensed by the State and registered with the City, and most plumbing work inside city limits has to be done by such a contractor. The office is in the City Hall Annex at 424 E. Hobson Ave., and it lists a rule that inspections requested before 9:00 am are generally done that business day.

The same page says work done without permits can be penalized at no less than double the permit and inspection fees. Practical takeaway: put the plumbing permit and rough-in inspection on the schedule before you book the concrete crew, and ask Building Inspections which stages must be inspected for your project. Also verify your plumber's state license with the Oklahoma Construction Industries Board.

Mistakes to avoid

Most slab regrets come from the same short list.

  1. Pouring before the inspector signs off, then arguing about who pays to open the floor
  2. Moving a fixture on the plan after the trenches are dug
  3. Letting backfill with large rocks or debris cover the pipe
  4. Skipping the cleanout because it "adds a fitting"
  5. Forgetting the floor drain or laundry drain until the framers ask where it is
  6. Not recording the routing

Where to take this next

If the slab belongs to a new home, your builder may already hold the permit, so ask for the plumber's name and the inspection date. If it is an addition, the work often has to tie into existing lines, which means someone should also check the condition of the pipe you are joining to. A licensed plumber can walk the site and explain the rough-in order for your job through plumbing installation service.

Homes in Kellyville, about 8 miles southwest of Sapulpa, and in Glenpool, a Tulsa suburb with its own city permit process, follow different local offices, so confirm which jurisdiction issues the permit before you plan dates.

Sources and standards

Under-slab rough-in follows the UPC or IPC as adopted locally for drain slope, pipe support and testing, and it is inspected before the pour, which is why the permit step comes first. Permit and edition questions belong to the city office, and license questions to the state board.
