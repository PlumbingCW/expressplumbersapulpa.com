Installing Baseboard Hot Water Heat in a Sapulpa, OK Home

Fin-tube baseboard looks simple on the wall. The decisions behind it are heat source, loop design and air removal.

Call (855) 575-2890

Three questions decide how a baseboard hot water heat install in Sapulpa, OK goes: what heats the water, how the pipe loops through the rooms, and how air gets out. Hydronic baseboard is a copper pipe wrapped in aluminum fins and set inside a metal cover along the wall. Warm water runs through the pipe, and air rising over the fins warms the room. The details are below.

Start with the heat source

The first fork in the road is where the hot water comes from. Each option changes the cost, the controls and who has to be involved.

Heat sourceHow it worksMain trade-off
Dedicated boilerA closed loop, separate from your drinking waterThe standard answer, but a new appliance to buy, vent and service
Existing boilerTie new baseboard loops into current pipingCheapest path if the boiler has spare capacity, which a heat loss check must confirm
Water heater as sourceUses a tank unit to feed a heating loopOnly if the maker lists it for space heating, and often needs a heat exchanger to protect drinking water
Heat pump or other sourceHeats water to a lower temperatureBaseboard emits less at low water temperatures, so rooms may need more footage

The water heater option deserves caution. Drinking water in a loop that sits for long periods can pick up metals and growth, and heating loops may contain additives. Many manufacturers prohibit certain uses, so a licensed plumber should confirm the unit's documents before anyone plans around it.

Loop design: how the pipe travels

Water loses heat as it flows. In a series loop, every baseboard sits in one long chain, so the last room gets cooler water and heats less. That is why a long series run is often split into two shorter loops.

A parallel layout feeds each loop from supply and return mains. It costs more in pipe, but the rooms heat more evenly and each loop can have its own thermostat. Many houses end up with a hybrid: one loop per zone and a short series run within each zone.

Pipe size also matters. Too small, and water moves too slowly to deliver heat. Too large, and the system costs more and responds slowly. The plumber matches pipe size, pump and loop length to the boiler's output.

How much baseboard goes where

Baseboard is rated in BTUs per hour per foot at a given water temperature. A room needs enough rated length to cover its heat loss on a cold day. Windows are the usual starting place, since fin tube under a window fights the cold air coming off the glass.

Furniture matters too. A sofa pushed against the cover blocks the airflow, and long curtains hanging over the cover do the same. A plan that fits on paper can fail if the layout leaves no clear wall.

Bleeding air: the part everyone meets

Air trapped in a loop makes it gurgle, tick and stay cold in spots. After the system is filled, air is purged zone by zone. Many systems have a purge valve at the mains. High points in the baseboard may have a small bleeder valve.

  1. 1Turn the boiler off and let the loop coolHot water can scald when a vent is opened.
  2. 2Set the room thermostat lowThe pump should not run while you work.
  3. 3Find the bleederIt is a small valve or screw near the top of the baseboard end cap or a high point in the pipe.
  4. 4Open it slightly with a hose or a rag readyAir hisses out first, then a little water.
  5. 5Close when water flows steadilyDo not keep it open once water appears.
  6. 6Check the gaugePressure should return to the value in your boiler manual. Add water through the fill valve only as the manual describes.

Zoning, thermostats and control

Each zone needs its own thermostat and a way to stop flow to other zones. Zone valves are the most common method on a single-pump system, and a separate small pump per zone is the other. The boiler's control board starts when any zone calls for heat.

Zoning is where a design earns or loses comfort. Bedrooms and a bathroom may want their own thermostats, while the hall can share with the living room. More zones add wiring and parts, so each one should have a reason.

What a homeowner can and cannot do

Some of this work is within reach of a careful owner. Removing old covers, painting, cleaning the fins with a vacuum brush and bleeding a bleeder valve as above are all common maintenance tasks. Straightening bent fins with a fin comb is another.

Cutting into pressurized pipe, soldering, connecting a boiler, making gas or vent connections and wiring the controls are different. A solder joint that weeps inside a wall causes damage. Gas and venting errors are dangerous. A licensed plumber should do these.
