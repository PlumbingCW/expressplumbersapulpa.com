What to do while you wait

  • Turn off the cold water supply valve on the pipe running into the top of the tank. A quarter-turn lever or a round handle, turned clockwise, stops the flow in.
  • Electric heater: switch off its double-pole breaker in the panel. Never drain an electric tank with the power on, because a dry element burns out in seconds.
  • Gas heater: turn the gas control knob to OFF or PILOT. If you smell gas, skip every other step and read the gas-safety block further down.
  • Move anything valuable off the floor around the tank and put a bucket or towels under a slow drip.
  • Do not touch a hot discharge pipe or a hot tank, and keep children and pets back. Water above about 120 degrees F can scald quickly.
  • Take a photo of the data plate (brand, model, serial, capacity) and of the leak, which helps with parts and with any insurance claim.

Symptoms people notice

Most calls start with one of a few complaints. Matching yours to the likely cause helps the plumber arrive with the right parts, even though nothing is confirmed until the heater is opened up.

No hot water at all

Electric: a tripped breaker, a tripped high-limit reset, or a failed thermostat or element. Gas: a dead pilot, a bad thermocouple, or no gas at the control valve.

Hot water runs out fast

A dead lower element, a broken dip tube, or sediment cutting tank volume. Showers start hot and turn cold in minutes.

Rumbling, popping or ticking

Scale on the tank floor traps steam bubbles that burst under the burner or element. The noise means heat transfer is poor.

Rusty or smelly water

Rust only in hot water often means a spent anode rod. A rotten-egg smell in hot water only points to bacteria feeding on the rod.

Water on the floor

A loose fitting, a weeping relief valve, condensation, or a failed tank. Water seeping from under the insulation is the one that ends in replacement.

How the fault is found

A diagnostic visit follows a sequence. The plumber confirms the fuel type and reads the data plate for age, since a manufacture date is built into the serial number. Then the test depends on the heater.

On an electric tank, the plumber cuts power, pulls the access panels, and checks each element and thermostat with a multimeter for continuity and resistance. On a gas tank, the checks are pilot flame color, thermocouple output in millivolts, the gas control valve, and flue draft.

For leaks, the plumber dries the area and traces water to a fitting, the T&P valve, the drain valve, or the tank. A pressure gauge on a hose bibb shows whether house pressure is too high.

Gas, electric and hybrid heat pump water heaters compared

The three types fail in different ways, so repair looks different too. Heat pump units, sold as hybrid water heaters, are newer and have parts that most plumbers rarely see on an old tank.

Gas tank heaters

Common repairs are a thermocouple or flame sensor, a pilot assembly, a gas control valve, a dirty burner, or a blocked flue. A backdrafting heater can push carbon monoxide into the home, so a working CO alarm nearby is a sensible safeguard.

Electric tank heaters

Two elements and two thermostats do the work. Elements fail from scale and dry firing, thermostats from age. These are usually quick repairs as long as the tank is sound.

Heat pump (hybrid) heaters

These pull heat from the surrounding air into the tank with a small compressor and refrigerant circuit, with electric elements as backup. Common service points are a clogged air filter, a blocked condensate drain, a failed fan, or a control board error. Error codes vary by brand, so the manufacturer's manual drives the repair.

Gas-safety block

  • Rotten-egg odor, hissing, or a gas smell near the heater: get everyone out of the house right away.
  • Do not flip light switches, use a lighter, or operate any electrical equipment on the way out.
  • Once you are safely away from the house, call 911, then call Oklahoma Natural Gas at 800-458-4251, or your gas utility if it is a different company.
  • Do not go back in until the utility or fire department says it is safe. A plumber cannot test for gas by phone, and we do not try.
  • A pilot that keeps going out, soot on the heater, or a yellow burner flame also needs a plumber, but not a rush out the door unless you smell gas.

Relief valves, anode rods and sediment

The temperature and pressure relief valve opens if the tank gets too hot or too pressurized. A valve that drips may be failing, or reacting to thermal expansion in a closed plumbing system, where an expansion tank may be the fix. Never cap or plug a discharge pipe.

The anode rod is a magnesium or aluminum rod that corrodes so the tank steel does not. Once it is used up, the tank itself starts to rust. Many manuals call for checking it every few years, and swapping a spent rod can extend a tank with no leak yet. Flushing sediment through the drain valve helps most before the buildup hardens.

What homeowner's insurance usually covers

Homeowner's policies often respond to sudden water damage, such as a tank that bursts and floods a room, but usually do not pay to replace a heater that simply wore out. Slow leaks and maintenance neglect are commonly excluded. Check your policy and call your insurer before cleanup beyond stopping the water. Photos, the data plate and the plumber's written findings are what most claims ask for.
