What a slab leak sounds, feels and looks like

Homeowners usually notice one of a handful of things first. A tile or hardwood floor feels warm in one spot, as if a heater were under it. A faint hiss or rushing sound comes from the floor in a quiet house. Carpet edges turn damp or smell musty, grout lines darken, or a baseboard starts to swell.

Others see it on paper, with a water bill that jumps or a meter that keeps moving after every fixture is closed. Low hot water pressure and a water heater that never seems to stop running point the same way. Any one of these is a reason to call, and two or three together make a slab leak likely.

Why pipes fail under a slab

Pipes in or beneath concrete are buried with no way to see them. Copper can pit from the inside or wear where it rubs against rough concrete as the house expands and contracts with heat and moisture. Older steel lines corrode. Plastic lines can fail at joints, and high water pressure speeds up every one of these problems.

Sapulpa's historic town center and its newer subdivisions mix many building eras, and we cannot say how many local homes are slab built. The practical point is that the plumber we send looks at what your house actually has, and will tell you the pipe material before recommending a repair.

How the leak is found under concrete

Locating a slab leak without breaking the floor is the first job. A visit usually starts with a meter test to confirm water is leaking and a pressure test to learn which line it is on.

  • Acoustic listening. Ground microphones pick up water escaping under pressure and locate it to a small area. Noisy surroundings and low-flow leaks can hide the sound.
  • Thermal imaging. A camera shows heat from a hot water line under the floor. It works best for hot leaks and cannot see through thick concrete on its own.
  • Line tracing and tracer gas. The plumber maps the pipe path, and tracer gas can pinpoint a stubborn leak. It is slower and used when other methods leave doubt.
  • Moisture meter. Readings show how far the water spread, but not the source.

Repair options and how they compare

There are three basic choices, and the right one depends on the pipe, how many leaks exist and where the lines run.

Spot repair

A plumber marks the spot, saws and jackhammers a small opening, repairs the pipe and patches the concrete. It is the least invasive path, but flooring over the cut may not match when the work is done. Ask whether patching the surface is part of the price.

Rerouting the line

Instead of digging, the plumber abandons the leaking run and installs a new one above the floor. It avoids breaking more concrete, but it is not invisible, since walls and soffits may need to be opened and closed.

Repiping

When pipe is old or has leaked more than once, replacing it can be a better choice than chasing one failure after another. See repiping for that decision. Ask the plumber to explain the evidence so you can judge it yourself.

What to do while you wait

  • Close the main water shutoff, then open a faucet to drain pressure.
  • Move rugs, furniture and electronics out of the wet or warm area.
  • Turn off power to a wet room only if the breaker is dry and reachable. Otherwise call 911.
  • Do not run the water heater with the supply off. Turn its power or gas control off.
  • Photograph wet floors, stains and the meter reading for your records.
  • If you smell gas, leave the house, call 911, then your gas utility.

Insurance and slab leaks

Policies differ on slab leaks. Some cover water damage from a sudden leak but not the pipe repair or the access cost, and long-term seepage is often treated differently. Check your policy and call your agent before work starts. Photos, the meter reading and the plumber's written findings help.
