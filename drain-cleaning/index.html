Slow, clogged, or backing up: what you are really looking at

Callers use these three words for different problems, and the difference tells a plumber where to start. A slow drain still empties, just with a gurgle or a lazy swirl. A clogged drain holds water in one fixture and does not move it at all. A backing-up drain pushes water or sewage out of a different opening than the one you used.

The pattern matters more than the word. One slow bathroom sink is almost always hair and soap scum in its own trap. Two or three fixtures acting up together point past the trap, to the branch line or the main.

What to do while you wait

  • Stop running water everywhere, including the dishwasher and washing machine.
  • Do not flush toilets if any drain is backing up.
  • Skip chemical drain openers. They sit in the pipe, burn skin, and make the plumber's job riskier.
  • Pull rugs and cardboard away from any wet floor and put towels at the edge of the spill.
  • Find the main cleanout if you can (a capped pipe, often outside near the foundation or in a basement or garage) and clear the space around it.
  • If sewage has reached the floor, keep kids and pets out of the room and wear rubber gloves for anything you touch.
  • Take photos of any water damage before you clean up.

Kitchen sink clogs

Kitchen sinks clog differently from bathroom sinks because grease is the main problem. Cooking oil goes down warm and liquid, then cools on the pipe wall and builds a waxy layer that catches rice, coffee grounds, pasta and eggshell bits. Over months the pipe narrows until a bowl of dishwater drains like a straw.

A double basin with a disposal adds a second trouble spot, the disposal outlet and its short tailpiece. If both sides of the sink are full, the blockage is past the joining tee. If only the disposal side holds water, the problem may be the disposal itself, and garbage disposal repair is the better fit.

A dishwasher that leaves dirty water in the bottom is another sign of a downstream clog, since its hose ties into the disposal or tailpiece. A plumber typically cleans the trap, runs a small cable through the wall stub-out, and checks the dishwasher high loop.

How a plumber finds and clears the blockage

A visit usually starts with questions, then a look. The plumber checks which fixtures are affected, runs water to watch the pattern, and opens the nearest cleanout or pulls a trap. From there the tool depends on the pipe.

A drum-style cable machine with a cutting head handles most household clogs. Small cables (about 1/4 to 3/8 inch) fit sink lines. Larger cables (1/2 inch and up) are used on 3 and 4 inch drain lines and the main. A toilet auger works on a closet bend without scratching the porcelain.

When the cable keeps hitting the same point, or the clog returns, a sewer camera shows what is actually there. A water-jetting head can follow for grease and heavy buildup, as explained on the hydro-jetting page. We do not promise a result by phone, and the visit price is stated before work starts.

Why a snake sometimes stops short

A snake that stalls partway is information, not just a nuisance. The cable is stopped by something, and the plumber has to work out what.

  • A root mass. Fine roots wrap the cable and hold it. A cutting head can open a channel, but the roots grow back.
  • A bend or fitting. A tight 90 degree turn or a mismatched coupling can catch the cable end.
  • A belly (sag) in the line. The cable coils in the low spot and cannot push forward, and water settles there.
  • A broken or offset pipe. The head drops into a gap or hits the edge of a shifted joint.
  • A solid obstruction. Toys, wipes, a wad of paper towel or a collapsed pipe section.

If the cable will not pass, the right next move is a camera, not more force. Pushing harder can damage old pipe. See sewer inspection for what a camera reveals.

Drain line leaks

A drain can leak as well as clog. A slow drip at a trap joint, a damp cabinet floor under the sink, or a stain on the ceiling below a bathroom often means a loose slip nut or a cracked fitting, not a supply problem. Drain leaks only show up when water is running down the line, so they appear and disappear.

A plumber checks by filling the fixture, letting it drain, and watching the joints and the pipe below. Hidden leaks inside a wall or under a slab call for leak detection instead.

When a clog means the main line

If the house has one problem, look at the fixture. If it has several, look at the main. The signs of a main-line blockage are gurgling in the lowest drains, a toilet that bubbles when the washer drains, and water rising in a shower or tub on the lowest floor. All fixtures share that last stretch of pipe before it leaves the house.

What decides the price of drain cleaning

A clear price is given before work starts. The final number depends on the job, so call for your real number.

A fixture trap is a quick job. A 4 inch main line behind the house needs bigger equipment and more time. The farther the clog from the access point, the more work.

A cleanout makes the job simple. Without one, the plumber may pull a toilet or work through a roof vent, which adds labor.

Hair and soap scum come out easily. Grease, roots and compacted paper take heavier cutting heads, and sometimes more than one pass.

Looking inside the line adds time but tells you whether cleaning is enough or the pipe needs repair.

Insurance note

Homeowner's policies often cover sudden water damage from a pipe failure but treat sewer backup as an optional add-on, and many policies exclude damage from slow neglect. Cleaning the drain itself is normally a maintenance cost the owner pays. Check your policy, call your insurer before cleanup if damage is large, and keep your photos and the plumber's invoice.
