A burst or spraying supply line

The flexible line between the wall valve and the faucet is a weak point, especially braided lines with aged rubber inside or old plastic couplings. When one splits, it can empty gallons a minute into a cabinet.

Close the little valve on the wall or floor, called an angle stop, by turning it clockwise. If it will not turn or keeps spraying, close the main shutoff. Empty the cabinet, mop up and run a fan. Photograph the damage before you clean.

Replacing a supply line is usually quick, but if the stop behind it is corroded the plumber replaces that too. The line itself is a wear item, so it is worth checking the others in the house while the plumber is there.

When the shutoff valve will not shut off

Valves that are rarely turned seize up. An old multi-turn stop can spin without closing, leak at the stem or snap when forced. Do not crank on a stuck valve with a wrench, since it can break and turn a drip into a flood.

If the stop fails, the main house valve is your backup. A plumber can replace the stop with a quarter-turn type, which closes fully with a short twist and is easier to trust. That is often done while the faucet is being fixed, since the water is already off.

Finding your main valve before an emergency saves time. It is often near the water heater, in a utility room or where the line enters the house. If the main also sticks, treat it as a priority repair and ask the dispatcher for help, not a project for later.

A faucet that drips or will not stop running

A steady drip wastes water and usually means a worn internal part. The fix depends on the type of faucet:

  • Compression faucets have separate hot and cold handles that screw down onto rubber washers. Worn washers or a pitted seat cause drips.
  • Cartridge faucets use a sealed cartridge that slides or rotates. A stiff handle, dribbling spout or water at the base often means the cartridge or its O-rings.
  • Ball faucets have a single handle over a rotating ball with springs and seals, which wear and leak at the spout or under the handle.
  • Ceramic disc faucets use two polished discs. They last, but mineral buildup or a cracked disc causes drips.

Parts are brand specific, so a plumber identifies the model before repairing it. If a faucet is old, its parts discontinued or the body corroded, replacement is the better call.

Leaks at the handle, spout or base

Water around the handle points to a worn packing or cartridge. A wet cabinet floor with a dry countertop suggests the supply connections. Water at the base of a spout is often a failed O-ring that swivels.

These are small repairs, but water inside a cabinet rots the floor of the cabinet and grows mold. Check under the sink with a flashlight and a dry paper towel pressed on each connection. If the towel is wet, shut off the stops and call.

For leaks you cannot trace, a moving water meter points to a bigger problem, covered on our leak detection page.

Tub and shower valves

A shower that drips after you turn it off, a handle that will not hold temperature, or a tub spout that sprays from the diverter all point to the valve behind the wall. Most are cartridge valves that can be swapped through the trim opening without breaking tile.

A pressure-balancing or thermostatic cartridge protects you from sudden hot or cold swings. If it fails, water can scald, so call when the temperature behaves oddly. If the valve body behind the wall leaks, access may require opening a wall, and the plumber explains how before cutting.

A shower valve that cannot be shut off is a true emergency. Close the main valve and call.

Replacing a faucet or fixture

Replacement makes sense when parts are unavailable, the finish has worn through, the body is corroded or you simply want a better fixture. Match the number of holes in the sink or counter, the spout reach and the supply connection style. A plumber also checks the stops and supply lines, replacing them if they are worn.

Kitchen sink faucets often come with a pull-down sprayer, and touchless models need power. Read the manufacturer's instructions for the model you buy. For a bigger fixture project, see our plumbing installation page.

A sink that leaks from the drain or trap, not the faucet, is another job. If the drain beneath a kitchen sink is slow, see garbage disposal repair as well.

Hose bibs and outdoor spigots

An outdoor hose bib that drips behind the handle, sprays when the hose is on or leaks inside the wall after winter is common. Frost-free types have a long stem that closes inside the warm part of the wall. If a hose is left attached in freezing weather, water trapped in the stem can freeze and split the pipe, and the leak shows when the weather warms.

Tulsa-area January lows average about 28 F, and the cold in February 2021 reached about negative 13 to 14 F, so disconnect hoses before winter and shut off any interior shutoff to the line. A plumber can replace the bib, add a vacuum breaker or repair the pipe behind it.

What to do while you wait

  • Turn the valve under the fixture clockwise. If it will not close, close the main water valve.
  • Empty the cabinet or area and set out towels and a bucket.
  • Keep electrical cords, power strips and appliances away from wet areas.
  • Do not force a stuck valve with a pipe wrench.
  • Photograph the leak, the fixture's make and model if visible, and the damage.
